Your home screen is a screen you look at dozens of times a day. It's the first thing you see every time you unlock the phone. That's exactly why what sits there has such a large influence on what you do next.

The principle is simple: **what's visible is what you end up doing.** If a social app's icon is on your home screen, you'll open it. If a news widget is there, you'll read it. When it isn't there, the number of times you go out of your way to open it drops. Here's how to build a home screen that doesn't get in the way of focus.

## 1. Keep only your "first string"

The first move is subtraction. Look at the icons on your home screen now and sort them by one question: **"Do I use this every single day?"** Phone, messages, camera, maps — those stay. Social, video, shopping, games are, for most people, not first-string.

Eight to ten icons on the first screen is plenty. Send the rest to the app drawer. **You don't have to uninstall anything.** Just removing them from the first screen sharply cuts how often you open them without thinking.

## 2. Bury the tempting apps two taps deep

Move the apps you tend to linger in — social, video — into a folder or onto a second page. The point is to **add friction by increasing the number of taps.**

People abandon an action more easily when it takes even one extra step. Turning it into three steps — "home → open folder → pick app" — shrinks the "open it out of habit" impulse and nudges you toward "open it only when I actually mean to."

## 3. Widgets for information, not for entrances

Widgets are handy, but choose them carefully. A good rule: **"read and done" information widgets are fine; "gateway" widgets that pull you into a rabbit hole are not.**

- Fine: clock, weather, calendar, battery, or a [progress-dots widget](/en/apps/progressdot/) — things you glance at and you're done.
- Avoid: social timelines, news feeds, video recommendations — anything where scrolling begins.

Keep widgets that let you check something; skip widgets that carry you off into another world. That one distinction does a lot of work.

## 4. Turn off notification badges

The little red number on an icon (the notification badge) is silent pressure to "open me now." When your brain sees an unread count, it wants to clear it.

In settings, turn off notification badges for non-urgent apps. Just making the badges disappear removes that "you're being paged" feeling when you look at the home screen, and it gets quieter.

## 5. Keep the wallpaper simple

Last, the wallpaper. A busy, vivid wallpaper is itself visual noise. A calm, near-solid-color background makes icons easier to read and makes the whole screen feel like "a place with breathing room" rather than a workbench.

To go further, try setting the screen to grayscale in the evening. Less color stimulation makes aimless browsing easier to stop.

## The option of switching launchers entirely

Everything above is about tidying the standard home screen, but there's another route: **swapping the home app (launcher) itself for one built around focus.**

For example, [TimeAware](/en/apps/timeaware/), which shows only a minimal set of apps, is built specifically to keep the home screen quiet. There are also launchers like [OneMotion Launcher](/en/apps/onemotion/), gesture-first by design, that don't line up icons at all. If tidying the standard home still doesn't feel calm, changing the foundation is worth a try.

## Wrapping up

The trick to a home screen that supports focus isn't adding features — it's **taking them away.** Keep the first string, bury the temptations, skip gateway widgets, kill the badges, quiet the wallpaper.

Once you've built it, you get a screen where, even after you open it, you fall straight back to the task at hand. Start by cutting the icons on your first screen in half.
